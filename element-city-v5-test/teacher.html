Field guide / classroom edition

A city that runs
on scientific thinking.

PTA: Periodic Table Auto is an original, browser-based chemistry investigation game. Students explore a 3D coastal city, borrow authorized City Share fleet cars, gather evidence, and restore services through six investigations that follow the unit: table structure → attraction → trends → metals and nonmetals → reactivity → acid/base OH compounds → ions.

Start playing

Select Join the response team, follow the movement and fleet-vehicle tutorial, and follow the gold route on the street and on the circular radar to the first investigation. Stop near a labeled station and interact. In the first investigation, accept Maya’s dispatch, exit the fleet car, and inspect the three labeled sample benches in the city. Run virtual current, flex, and compression tests, compare the evidence, carry a sample, connect terminal A then B, and run the flex cycle. The street signals change when the repair succeeds. Later investigations retain their original panel interfaces.

Dispatch includes an optional city shuttle to the current investigation. There is no penalty for thinking slowly. Driving and exploration achievements are separate from chemistry progress. A helpful wrong answer is part of an investigation, not a dead end.

Computer keyboard

  • WASD or arrow keys: walk. In a vehicle, up/down accelerate or brake/reverse; left/right steer.
  • E: enter or exit a nearby city fleet vehicle.
  • F: interact at a nearby station.
  • T: periodic table. N: field notebook. M: city map.
  • Esc: pause / close a panel. Use the visible tool buttons for the same actions.

iPad touch controls and recovery

On touch devices the on-screen controls appear automatically. Put your left thumb anywhere in the lower-left area and slide: a thumbstick appears under it to walk or steer. In the car, hold GAS with your right thumb while steering with your left; BRAKE stops the car and, if held, reverses. Drag the city with another finger to look (sideways turns, up/down tilts). Recenter restores the follow camera and Camera switches to an overhead view. The camera shortens near buildings. Pause offers arrow-button controls, control size, camera sensitivity, graphics quality (Automatic, High, Balanced, Battery saver) and the street guide line.

Landscape gives the city and controls more room. Pause before switching apps or handing the iPad to a partner. Keyboard controls remain available on computers or with an attached keyboard.

The pause menu provides recovery if you become stuck, mission restart, audio and motion settings, and a clearly labeled progress reset. Resetting progress removes this browser's saved game.

Touch support is implemented; physical school-iPad behavior still requires classroom testing.

The notebook and HQ also offer three optional research activities: periodic-trend explanations, interpretation of an aqueous monitoring record, and neutral ion ratios. These use original situations and are tracked separately from the five city investigations.

Suggested classroom flow

  1. Launch, 2–3 minutes: demonstrate the touch pad, simultaneous forward-and-steer driving, interacting, and opening the periodic table. Explain that samples and testing systems are virtual.
  2. Investigate, approximately 20–30 minutes: students work individually or in pairs. One student navigates while the other checks evidence; switch roles between missions.
  3. Debrief, 5 minutes: students use their progress summary and notebook to explain one successful revision with evidence.

These are planning estimates, not measured playtest results. Reading speed, discussion, exploration, and device familiarity will change completion time. Pauses and slower play do not block chemistry content.

Investigation sequence

1 · Who Am I? (courier run)Read a “Who am I?” clue (group/period, family, valence electrons + energy levels, proton count), find the element, and deliver to the building wearing that rooftop tile.What does the group tell you about valence electrons? 3 · Power to the Block (street workshop)Run look / bend / conduct tests on copper, silicon, sulfur and graphite; classify each by position; build a flexible cable and justify it with two properties.Why is one property never enough? 4 · Fountain Show (harbor)Lock in a least → most reactive order for Li/Na/K, then Na/Mg/Al, then Mg/Ca/Sr/Ba; watch the virtual show; revise; choose the explanation.How does ionization energy predict reactivity? Why do Na → Mg → Al get less reactive?

Why Al₂O₃ and not AlO? Why doesn’t K₂S behave like potassium or sulfur?

How the game teaches

  • Predict, then see evidence. Orders and answers are locked in before the fountain show runs or the arch is passed.
  • Hints never give the answer. Each ladder ends with a worked example that uses different elements.
  • Scoring. +100 REP per investigation and +10 for each question answered right on the first try. Wrong attempts never earn points; hints are free.
  • Fun extras. Three original car-radio stations (generated live, off by default; Q or the radio button), six borrowable City Share cars, and hidden element coins.

Learning goals and evidence of progress

Students practice locating elements by atomic number, symbol, group, and period; recognizing Group 1, 2, 17, and 18 families; comparing metals, nonmetals, and metalloids; using material-property evidence; explaining introductory periodic trends; and balancing simple ionic formulas.

The field notebook and student progress summary record completed investigations and concepts practiced. Completion is evidence of participation and successful in-game decisions, not a validated measure of independent mastery. Ask students to explain their reasoning separately before using completion for assessment.

Three useful exit prompts

  1. Describe a prediction you changed. What evidence changed your mind?
  2. Use both table position and observed properties to support a material identification.
  3. Explain why the properties of an element do not automatically become the properties of a compound containing it.

Scientific models and deliberate simplifications

  • Conditions: ordinary reference states refer to elemental substances near 20–25 °C and 1 atmosphere. State depends on conditions.
  • Identity: atomic number is the number of protons. Neutral atoms, ions, elemental substances, and compounds are different ideas.
  • Families: hydrogen is a nonmetal placed above Group 1, not an alkali metal. Noble gases generally have low reactivity; some form compounds. Family members need not have identical properties.
  • Categories: conductivity, luster, and malleability are evidence patterns, not infallible single tests. Graphite conducts. Not all metals are magnetic. Silicon's conductivity depends on conditions.
  • Trends: periodic trends are general patterns with exceptions. Shell pictures are simplified energy-level models, not literal electron orbits or scale drawings. Virtual response bars are qualitative representations, not measured rates.
  • Ions: common Group 1, Group 2, and halogen charge patterns are applied to appropriate simple main-group ions. Transition-metal charges vary. Ionic formulas give lowest whole-number ratios in a lattice, not discrete molecules.
  • Table layout: this edition shows La and Ac in Group 3 with detached Ce–Lu and Th–Lr continuations of periods 6 and 7. Alternative Group 3 layouts exist. Metalloids have boundary conventions; Zn, Cd, and Hg appear under “Other metal.”
  • Uncertainty: uncertain bulk properties of short-lived elements are labeled unknown. Group position does not prove that oganesson is a gas.
  • OH compounds and indicator: the survey uses the classroom universal-indicator scale (red ≤3, orange–yellow 4–5, green 7, blue-green/blue 8–9, purple ≥10) and the lab’s OH-formula notation (SO₂(OH)₂, PO(OH)₃, CO(OH)₂). Mg(OH)₂ is shown after heating; Al(OH)₃ shows no visible change and belongs in “not sure.” Records are virtual; no procedure is given.
  • Visual scale: the city is drawn at about 15 world units per metre; displayed speeds are converted with that scale. Buildings are taller than their gameplay footprints imply so the skyline reads as a real downtown.
  • Fiction: characters, infrastructure failures, city response meters, rewards, and the ion-balancing device are fictional. All reactive-material testing is virtual and gives no real-world experiment procedure.

Classroom access and privacy

The static game needs no account, paid API, or AI model. Game progress stays in local browser storage when available; if storage is blocked, play can continue without persistent saving. A private-browsing session, browser-data cleanup, or a different device may lose or separate progress. The game does not request a student's name, location, email, microphone, or camera and includes no analytics.

The game uses keyboard-operable HTML menus, text alongside category colors, large touch controls, pauseable reading, optional audio, and a reduced-motion setting. The visual Canvas city is not fully screen-reader accessible. No claim is made of full assistive-technology or all-device compatibility.

Before classroom rollout: check a physical school iPad in both orientations; simultaneous steering and acceleration; Safari scrolling and overlays; app switching; managed-browser local storage; volume behavior; and any school content filtering. Review contrast and text scale on the actual classroom displays.
