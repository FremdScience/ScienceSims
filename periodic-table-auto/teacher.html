Teacher and player guide

A city that runs
on scientific thinking.

PTA I: Element City is an original, browser-based chemistry investigation game. Students explore a 3D coastal city, borrow authorized City Share fleet cars, gather evidence, and restore services through six investigations that follow a typical first-year chemistry periodic-table sequence: table structure → periodic trends (atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity) → metals, nonmetals and metalloids → reactivity of metals → acid/base behavior of hydroxides and oxyacids → ions and formulas. Investigations unlock in order; progress saves on the device.

Start playing

Select Join the response team, follow the movement and fleet-vehicle tutorial, and follow the gold route on the street and on the circular radar to the first investigation. Stop near a labeled station and interact. In the first investigation, meet Noor at the courier stand, read each “Who am I?” clue, and deliver the package to the building whose rooftop tile shows that element. Later investigations add a checkpoint race, a street workshop with four sample benches (look, bend, conduct), a harbor fountain show, a city-wide indicator survey and three ion relay towers.

Dispatch includes an optional city shuttle to the current investigation. There is no penalty for thinking slowly. Driving and exploration achievements are separate from chemistry progress. A helpful wrong answer is part of an investigation, not a dead end.

Computer keyboard

  • WASD or arrow keys: walk. In a vehicle, up/down accelerate or brake/reverse; left/right steer.
  • E: enter or exit a nearby city fleet vehicle.
  • F: interact at a nearby station.
  • T: periodic table. N: field notebook. M: city map.
  • Esc: pause / close a panel. Use the visible tool buttons for the same actions.

iPad touch controls and recovery

On touch devices the on-screen controls appear automatically. Put your left thumb anywhere in the lower-left area and slide: a thumbstick appears under it to walk or steer. In the car, hold GAS with your right thumb while steering with your left; BRAKE stops the car and, if held, reverses. Drag the city with another finger to look (sideways turns, up/down tilts). Recenter restores the follow camera and Camera switches to an overhead view. The camera shortens near buildings. Pause offers arrow-button controls, control size, camera sensitivity, graphics quality (Automatic, High, Balanced, Battery saver) and the street guide line.

Landscape gives the city and controls more room. Pause before switching apps or handing the iPad to a partner. Keyboard controls remain available on computers or with an attached keyboard.

The pause menu provides recovery if you become stuck, mission restart, audio and motion settings, and a clearly labeled progress reset. Resetting progress removes this browser's saved game.

Touch support is implemented; physical school-iPad behavior still requires classroom testing.

The notebook and HQ also offer three optional research activities: periodic-trend explanations, interpretation of an aqueous monitoring record, and neutral ion ratios. These use original situations and are tracked separately from the six city investigations.

Suggested flow and pacing

  1. Launch, about 5 minutes: demonstrate walking, simultaneous forward-and-steer driving, interacting, and opening the periodic table. Explain that samples and testing systems are virtual.
  2. Investigate: plan roughly 10–15 minutes per investigation, about two 45–50 minute periods for all six. Students work individually or in pairs. One student navigates while the other checks evidence; switch roles between investigations.
  3. Debrief, 5–10 minutes: students use their progress summary and notebook to explain one successful revision with evidence.

How the game teaches

  • Predict, then see evidence. Orders and answers are locked in before the fountain show runs or the arch is passed.
  • Hints never give the answer. Each ladder ends with a worked example that uses different elements.
  • A full reference table. Tap any element for its average atomic mass, valence electrons (main groups), electrons per energy level and full electron configuration (elements 1–36), noble-gas shorthand for every element, and Pauling electronegativity (elements 1–56, noble gases omitted). Groups are labeled 1–18 and 1A–8A.
  • Scoring. +100 REP per investigation and +10 for each question answered right on the first try. Wrong attempts never earn points; hints are free. Exploration adds a little more: +5 per element coin (+50 for all 18) and up to 300 from optional Element Express side jobs, so read REP and rank titles as engagement, not as a chemistry grade. The progress download lists first-try results per investigation.
  • Element Express. Optional “Who am I?” delivery side jobs that start from HQ after Investigation 01. An optional timer can be turned on in Pause; investigations are never timed.
  • Fun extras. Three original car-radio stations (generated live, off by default; Q or the radio button), six borrowable City Share cars, a black Carbon C6 as the starting fleet car, a hidden blue Cesium Cs-55 that unlocks after Investigation 01 (clue: the harbor named for Group 17; it is the fastest car in the city), and hidden element coins. The cars are original low-poly models without badges or logos.

Learning goals and evidence of progress

Students practice locating elements by atomic number, symbol, group, and period; recognizing Group 1, 2, 17, and 18 families; comparing metals, nonmetals, and metalloids; using material-property evidence; explaining introductory periodic trends; and balancing simple ionic formulas.

The field notebook and student progress summary record completed investigations and concepts practiced. Completion is evidence of participation and successful in-game decisions, not a validated measure of independent mastery. Ask students to explain their reasoning separately before using completion for assessment.

Three useful exit prompts

  1. Describe a prediction you changed. What evidence changed your mind?
  2. Use both table position and observed properties to support a material identification.
  3. Explain why the properties of an element do not automatically become the properties of a compound containing it.

Adapting to your course

Same idea, different words

  • Energy levels = electron shells.
  • Many US tables number main groups 1A–8A; the A number equals the valence electrons (helium has 2). The game shows both 1–18 and 1A–8A.
  • OH compounds = metal hydroxides (basic) and oxyacids (acidic), e.g. SO₂(OH)₂ = H₂SO₄.
  • The staircase = the metalloid zigzag line.
  • Protons vs. distance = nuclear charge vs. shielding (effective nuclear charge).
  • Electron configurations are written in filling order (4s before 3d); some texts write 3d first.
  • If your indicator chart differs, the color order is what matters.

Student glossary

  • REP: reputation points. +100 per investigation, +10 for each answer right on the first try, +5 per element coin and some for Element Express side jobs; hints are free and wrong answers never cost points.
  • City Share car: an authorized fleet car any player may borrow.
  • OH compound: a metal hydroxide such as NaOH (basic) or a nonmetal oxyacid such as SO₂(OH)₂ = H₂SO₄ (acidic).
  • Amphoteric: able to react with both acids and bases, like Al(OH)₃.
  • Electronegativity: how strongly an atom pulls on electrons it shares in a bond.

Scientific models and deliberate simplifications

  • Conditions: ordinary reference states refer to elemental substances near 20–25 °C and 1 atmosphere. State depends on conditions.
  • Identity: atomic number is the number of protons. Neutral atoms, ions, elemental substances, and compounds are different ideas.
  • Families: hydrogen is a nonmetal placed above Group 1, not an alkali metal. Noble gases generally have low reactivity; some form compounds. Family members need not have identical properties.
  • Categories: conductivity, luster, and malleability are evidence patterns, not infallible single tests. Graphite conducts. Not all metals are magnetic. Silicon's conductivity depends on conditions.
  • Trends: periodic trends are general patterns with exceptions. Electronegativity uses Pauling values; halogen reactivity is compared with Cl vs. Br, which avoids the fluorine electron-affinity anomaly. Shell pictures are simplified energy-level models, not literal electron orbits or scale drawings. Virtual response bars are qualitative representations, not measured rates.
  • Ions: common main-group charges (Group 1 +1, Group 2 +2, Group 13 +3, Group 15 −3, Group 16 −2, Group 17 −1) are applied to simple monatomic ions. Transition-metal charges vary (Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺). Ionic formulas give lowest whole-number ratios in a lattice, not discrete molecules.
  • Reference data: average atomic masses are IUPAC standard atomic weights (abridged; elements with none show —). Electron configurations follow filling order with the known exceptions (for example Cr [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ and Cu [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰); elements 104 and up are marked predicted.
  • Table layout: this edition shows La and Ac in Group 3 with detached Ce–Lu and Th–Lr continuations of periods 6 and 7. Alternative Group 3 layouts exist. Metalloids have boundary conventions; Zn, Cd, and Hg appear under “Other metal.”
  • Uncertainty: uncertain bulk properties of short-lived elements are labeled unknown. Group position does not prove that oganesson is a gas.
  • OH compounds and indicator: the survey uses this game’s indicator chart, one common universal-indicator color chart (red pH 0–3, orange–yellow 4–6, green about 7, blue-green/blue 8–9, purple 10–14; brands differ slightly), and every board cell shows the color name and pH band so the survey works without color vision. It writes oxyacids in structural OH form (SO₂(OH)₂ = H₂SO₄, PO(OH)₃ = H₃PO₄, CO(OH)₂ = H₂CO₃) so students can compare them with metal hydroxides. “Very acidic” describes a low pH reading, not acid strength: H₃PO₄ is a weak acid. Mg(OH)₂ is shown after heating and dissolves only slightly; Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric, shows no visible change, and belongs in “not sure.” Records are virtual; no procedure is given.
  • Visual scale: the city is drawn at about 15 world units per metre; displayed speeds are converted with that scale. Buildings are taller than their gameplay footprints imply so the skyline reads as a real downtown.
  • Fiction: characters, infrastructure failures, city response meters, rewards, and the ion-balancing device are fictional. All reactive-material testing is virtual and gives no real-world experiment procedure.

Classroom access and privacy

The static game needs no account, paid API, or AI model. Game progress stays in local browser storage when available; if storage is blocked, play can continue without persistent saving. A private-browsing session, browser-data cleanup, or a different device may lose or separate progress. The game does not request a student's name, location, email, microphone, or camera and includes no analytics.

The game uses keyboard-operable HTML menus, text alongside category colors, large touch controls, pauseable reading, optional audio, and a reduced-motion setting. The visual Canvas city is not fully screen-reader accessible. No claim is made of full assistive-technology or all-device compatibility.

Before classroom rollout: check a physical school iPad in both orientations; simultaneous steering and acceleration; Safari scrolling and overlays; app switching; managed-browser local storage; volume behavior; and any school content filtering. Review contrast and text scale on the actual classroom displays.
